# patearn — Failure Case Studies
## 6 Indian mid-cap failures with anti-signals present before each collapse

**How to use this file:** When a stock scores > 65% NS in the framework, run an adversarial check against these failure patterns. Ask: does this stock carry any of the same anti-signals that were present before these companies collapsed? If yes, re-examine the relevant Hard Disqualifier or Red Flag before proceeding.

The anti-signals listed were not discoverable only in hindsight. They were present in public documents — annual reports, BSE filings, auditor reports — before the price collapsed. They were ignored because the narrative was too compelling. The framework exists to prevent that.

---

## CASE 1 — Vakrangee Ltd (2016–2018) · Governance Fraud

**What looked right:**
- Revenue growing 40–50% CAGR
- ROE appeared high (30%+)
- Riding Digital India / financial inclusion tailwind
- Stock ran from ₹5 to ₹500 in 4 years — appeared to validate the thesis

**What actually happened:**
Revenue was largely fictitious — inflated through related parties and non-existent franchisees. Cash flow from operations consistently lagged reported PAT. Auditors had flagged concerns. Stock crashed 95% in 2018.

**Anti-signals present in public documents before the collapse:**
1. CFO/PAT ratio was consistently below 0.4 for 3 consecutive years — the clearest possible signal that profits were not real
2. Auditor qualifications and emphasis of matter appeared in the annual report before the crash
3. No clear explanation for high CWIP relative to a technology-light business model
4. Revenue per franchisee implied by disclosed numbers was implausibly high vs industry norms

**Framework lesson:**
When CFO is less than half of PAT for 3 consecutive years in a supposed high-growth business, the profits are not real. No tailwind justifies ignoring this. This is a Hard Disqualifier (Pattern 3 in the Disqualifier list).

**Applicable anti-signal check for any high-scoring stock:**
If NS > 65% but Pattern 2 (Operating Leverage) is scoring high — verify that the EPS growth is supported by CFO, not just reported PAT.

## CASE 2 — Manpasand Beverages (2017–2019) · Governance Fraud

**What looked right:**
- Rural FMCG compounder narrative in an under-penetrated market
- Revenue growing 25%+ CAGR
- Institutional investors (including reputed funds) entered in 2016–17
- Stock peaked above ₹500 in 2018

**What actually happened:**
Auditors Deloitte resigned mid-year in 2019 without completing the audit — the most unambiguous possible signal. Revenue and receivables were manipulated. Stock crashed 90%+ within months of the auditor departure.

**Anti-signals present in public documents before the collapse:**
1. Receivable days were rising 20–25 days every year for 3 years before the crash — visible on Screener.in
2. Auditor change was public information, dated, and visible in BSE filings before the full collapse
3. Low CFO relative to rapid PAT growth — 3-year CFO/PAT ratio < 0.5
4. Retail distribution claims were not verifiable from disclosed dealer/distributor data

**Framework lesson:**
Auditor resignation is a non-negotiable exit trigger, regardless of what management says about "differences of opinion." There is no such thing as a routine auditor departure in a high-growth company. This fires the Hard Disqualifier immediately.

**Applicable anti-signal check:**
If any stock has changed auditors in the last 3 years — check the reason, the date, and whether the departure was mid-year or at year-end. A mid-year departure is a Hard Disqualifier.

## CASE 3 — Suzlon Energy (2007–2012) · Leverage Trap

**What looked right:**
- Structurally correct sector (renewable energy) globally
- Revenue growing rapidly with global footprint
- Policy tailwinds in India and Europe
- Stock reached ₹450+ at the 2008 peak

**What actually happened:**
Acquired REpower (Germany) in a debt-funded deal that pushed Net D/E above 5×. When global financial crisis hit and order books slowed, interest servicing consumed all EBITDA. The business was operationally real — the debt was the killer.

**Anti-signals present in public documents before the collapse:**
1. Net D/E crossed 2× as early as 2006 and kept rising — visible in annual balance sheets
2. Acquisition at peak valuations in a cyclical industry — debt funded at the top of the cycle
3. Interest cost exceeded 15% of revenue — a hard warning level for a manufacturing company
4. Foreign currency debt exposure added additional risk not visible in INR standalone accounts

**Framework lesson:**
Structurally correct sector + right management + real business can still destroy shareholders when the balance sheet is weaponised through acquisitions at cycle peak. D/E > 2× rising = Hard Disqualifier. The sector narrative does not override this.

**Applicable anti-signal check:**
For any company in a capital-intensive sector (power, infra, manufacturing), always stress-test debt serviceability against a 30–40% revenue decline before entry. If EBIT/Interest drops below 2× under that scenario, the debt level is dangerous regardless of current coverage.

## CASE 4 — Aban Offshore (2007–2016) · Leverage Trap

**What looked right:**
- Offshore drilling — a massive global capex cycle was underway
- Revenue and EBITDA growing strongly through 2007–08
- Hard assets (jackup rigs) appeared to support the balance sheet
- Promoter stake was high and rising — appeared to be a positive signal

**What actually happened:**
Borrowed heavily to buy jackup rigs at peak oil prices (~$100/barrel cycle). When oil crashed to $30 in 2014–16, rig utilisation and day rates collapsed. Debt was fixed; EBITDA was not. Near-bankruptcy followed.

**Anti-signals present in public documents before the collapse:**
1. Net D/E was above 4× at the acquisition peak — deeply in Hard Disqualifier territory
2. Asset-heavy model with commodity-priced revenues: EBITDA was entirely cyclical, debt was permanent
3. Interest coverage had fallen below 2× before oil crashed — already in danger zone
4. High promoter stake was misread as confidence — but promoters were not reducing debt despite ability to

**Framework lesson:**
Hard assets on a balance sheet do not protect you when those assets lose their earning power. The value of a jackup rig depends entirely on oil prices, just as the value of a thermal power plant depends on merchant rates. Always stress-test: what is the asset worth at 30% lower utilisation or commodity price?

**Applicable anti-signal check:**
For any asset-heavy company (EPC, offshore, power, shipping), always check: at what utilisation or commodity price does EBIT cover interest at 1×? If the answer is "close to current levels," the company is already in a fragile position.

## CASE 5 — PC Jeweller (2017–2018) · Governance / Promoter Exit

**What looked right:**
- Organised jewellery — clear premiumisation story in a fragmented market
- Rapid store expansion across India
- Revenue CAGR 30%+ over 3 years
- Stock ran from ₹30 to ₹500+ by 2018 — appeared to validate the thesis

**What actually happened:**
Promoters announced and then cancelled a buyback (a classic price management signal). They then conducted an OFS at high prices. Governance concerns surfaced. Stock crashed 80%+.

**Anti-signals present in public documents before the collapse:**
1. Buyback announced and cancelled — the announcement serves to support price; the cancellation suggests the intent was never to buy at the stated levels
2. Promoter OFS at high price shortly after the buyback announcement — net promoter selling while retail investors were holding
3. Related party transactions with jewellery trading entities were high relative to revenue
4. Receivable days were elevated and included related-party receivables

**Framework lesson:**
A buyback announcement followed by OFS is not a confidence signal — it is an exit signal. Track promoter net buying vs net selling on a rolling 12-month basis. An OFS that reduces promoter stake > 5% in one transaction warrants immediate reassessment of the thesis.

**Applicable anti-signal check:**
Any stock where promoters have conducted an OFS in the last 24 months while simultaneously talking up the business story — require a higher bar of evidence for Pattern 8 (Promoter Accumulation). Flag the OFS explicitly in the bear case.

## CASE 6 — Yes Bank (2019–2020) · Financial / NPA Fraud

**What looked right:**
- Fast-growing private sector bank
- Revenue and advances growing 30%+ CAGR
- Management presented strong NIM and growth narrative
- Major institutions held large positions — appeared to validate quality

**What actually happened:**
RBI's inspection found actual NPAs were far higher than reported (divergence). Governance issues with the founder. RBI imposed moratorium in March 2020. Stock fell 95%.

**Anti-signals present in public documents before the collapse:**
1. RBI publicly flagged NPA divergence in its annual inspection report — public information
2. Advances growing much faster than deposits — structural funding mismatch that creates vulnerability
3. CEO under regulatory scrutiny for months before market fully reacted
4. Concentration in large corporate loans to stressed sectors (NBFC, real estate, infrastructure) was visible in quarterly disclosures

**Framework lesson:**
For financial companies (banks, NBFCs, insurance), the loan book quality IS the business model. When the regulator publicly flags NPA divergence or asset quality concerns, it is a Hard Disqualifier equivalent — exit immediately, regardless of the growth narrative or institutional ownership.

**Applicable anti-signal check:**
For any financial company, always check:
- RBI annual inspection report for any divergence notes
- Advance growth vs deposit growth ratio (> 1.3× sustained = funding risk)
- GNPA and NNPA trend for any acceleration
- Exposure to stressed sectors (real estate, NBFC loans) as % of total book

## SUMMARY — PRE-ENTRY ANTI-SIGNAL CHECKLIST

Before entering any position scoring > 65% NS, run through this 10-question adversarial check:

1. Is CFO/PAT ratio > 0.7 over the last 3 years?
2. Has the auditor been the same firm for 3+ years with no qualification?
3. Is D/E below 1.5× with interest coverage > 4×?
4. Is there any promoter OFS in the last 24 months?
5. Have receivable days been stable (no year-on-year increase > 10 days)?
6. Are related party transactions < 10% of revenue and arm's-length?
7. Is this a commodity-revenue, debt-heavy model (Aban/Suzlon risk)?
8. For financials: are NPAs consistent with RBI disclosures?
9. Has management's guidance matched delivery for 6 of last 8 quarters?
10. Is there any unexplained CWIP build-up in a non-capital-intensive business?

Any "No" answer triggers mandatory deep-dive into that specific point before position entry. Two or more "No" answers = do not enter until resolved.
